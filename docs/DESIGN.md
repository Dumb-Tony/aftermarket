# Aftermarket — design notes

Concept-stage notes for the prototype in `index.html`. Not a spec.

## Thesis

Most build games ask *what do I want to be good at*. Aftermarket asks *what am I going to do with all this heat*.

The robot constantly produces things it cannot use. A build is the plumbing you bolt on to decide where those things drain. One production rule governs the whole catalogue: **no part is neutral** — every part either makes a resource or drinks one. If you can't say which in four words, it doesn't ship.

## Resources

| | |
|---|---|
| **Heat** | Rises when you act. Falls when you stop or vent. At 100% the chassis force-vents: stagger, armour offline. Some cores read the bar as a damage multiplier instead. |
| **Recoil** | Not stored — an impulse applied the instant a shot leaves the barrel. Mass and leg type decide whether it's absorbed, converted to momentum, or turned into spin. |
| **Scrap** | Physical piles on the floor. Hot for 20s, then cold. Hot scrap is fuel; cold scrap is money. Same object, two economies. |

| **Charge** | Generated by moving — a flat trickle plus a kinetic bonus that scales with speed. The only currency that pays for an active utility. Jammers exist to cut it. |

Cores decide what those four *mean*. A Furnace reads heat as a damage multiplier; a Capacitor reads heat leaving the chassis as charge arriving; a Governor reads hot scrap as charge; a Dynamo reads speed as charge; a Ballast reads recoil as something that happens to other people.

## The three anchor builds

They are not separated by damage type. They're separated by where they want to stand relative to their own wreckage.

**Furnace Brawler** — heat never auto-vents; it sits in the core as a short-range damage multiplier, fed by hot scrap. Walks into the pile. Dies to tankers and to anything with range. Every fight is a bet on how long you can stay hot.

**Recoil Skater** — near-zero friction. Walking is miserable; shooting is how you move. Fastest thing on the floor, can't stop, can't use floor scrap. Every trigger pull is two choices: what dies, and where you end up.

**Drone Commander** — a Governor Core turns hot scrap into charge and charge into drones. Drones eat wreckage to repair themselves, and a drone that has eaten three times builds another drone. You are a logistics problem with a sidearm, and you are choosing the room's geometry rather than your own position.

## The workshop problem

The failure mode is a player opening four tooltips, reading two hundred words of stat deltas, equipping whatever has the biggest number, and leaving. The fix isn't shorter tooltips — it's refusing to make understanding a reading task.

1. **Schematic, not list.** The build is drawn as pipes between mounts.
2. **One line, one icon.** Five-word tagline per part. Numbers live behind a toggle.
3. **There is always something to shoot.** The arena stays live and a dummy respawns in it. The feel *is* the description.
4. **Swapping is free; owning costs.** Remove the fear and people experiment.
5. **Dead ends are drawn, not warned.** A resource with no consumer sparks. A sink shows a drain.
6. **Hovering redraws the robot.** Compare silhouettes, not paragraphs.
7. **No rarity colours.** Parts are coloured by the resource they touch, so you shop by what your circuit is missing.

## Palette law

Nothing on screen flashes in a colour that isn't a resource. Heat is orange, kinetic is bone, scrap is brass. Every effect is self-labelling, which means the feedback vocabulary teaches itself in thirty seconds.

## Open questions

- Is health *parts* (enemies shoot your build off you) or just health? Dramatic, thematic, possible death spiral.
- How much recoil is fun for the builds that aren't skaters? One tuning value probably decides whether the premise works.
- Does cooling scrap read as forward pressure or as anxiety?
- How many parts before the schematic stops being readable? Guess: 60–80.
- Is mismatched-arm torque too punishing as a universal rule, or should it be opt-in via a leg part?

## What the prototype measures

Will a player voluntarily rebuild once they discover heat can be a thruster — without being told to? Everything else is colour.


## Balance, measured

A headless bot plays each build identically on Contender: aim at the nearest thing, kite, close in if the build wants to be close, use the leg action and both utilities on a timer. Three runs each, average wave reached. The bot is mediocre on purpose — it reads *relative* build strength, not skill ceiling.

| build | avg wave |
|---|---|
| furnace + two Coolant Cells (the trap) | 1.7 |
| stock / rifle / walker (baseline) | 5.3 |
| furnace / lance / skates / vent | 7.7 |
| governor / intake / drone bays | 8.0 |
| dynamo / mortar / treads | 9.0 |
| capacitor / arc / piston | 9.7 |
| ballast / twin slugs / treads | 9.7 |
| ballast / slug / skates | 13.0 |

What this is for: the trap build must sit clearly below the baseline, coherent builds clearly above it, and the spread between the best and worst coherent build must stay inside about 2×. It does. Re-run it after any tuning pass.

Things the measurement caught that playing did not: salvage picks never refreshed after the first workshop, so a whole run could only ever draft one part; the furnace brawler cooked itself to death on wave one because a Thermal Lance filled its heat bar faster than the vent cooled it; and stacked damage reduction (Ballast core × Treads × brace) made one build effectively immortal.

## What is deliberately still missing

Co-op — designed for, not built. Precision dismemberment (how you kill deciding whether you get an intact part or raw scrap) is described above and not yet implemented. Parts being shot off you mid-wave is still the open question it was; nothing in this build degrades your chassis while you are using it.


## Making it a game rather than a system

Three things turned the prototype into something with a shape.

**An ending.** Three acts, fifteen waves, and The Inheritor — a champion wearing everything that lost, which fights you as each of the three archetype bosses in turn as its health drops. A roguelite without a finish line is a toy; one with a finish line and an endless mode past it gives the player somewhere to be going and somewhere to go afterwards.

**Contracts.** Fourteen parts are available from the start; seventeen are locked behind things you do — beat a specific champion, overheat fifteen times, build twenty-five drones, win on Champion. Offers only draw from the unlocked pool, so the design space opens as you play instead of drowning a new player in thirty-one parts they can't evaluate. It also means the anti-synergy lessons arrive in an order.

**A score that listens.** The music is a step sequencer, not a loop: a low pulse and a plant drone when you're cold, and as your heat bar climbs the filter opens and an arpeggio comes in over the top. Boss waves shift tempo and add a tritone stab; the workshop drops to a filtered pad. The point is that the thing the whole game is about — the heat bar — is audible without looking at it.

## Balance, second pass

Re-running the harness after the depth pass surfaced two things worth recording.

The Slag Vent used to be free and on a short cooldown, which quietly cancelled the cost of every hot weapon in the game — a twin-cannon turret could dump its heat forever and never pay for it. Making the vent cost charge fixed the turret and broke the furnace brawler, which depends on venting and generates little charge. The resolution was better than either: the vent now costs 10 charge and refunds up to 14 depending on how hot you were. Venting at the redline is nearly free; venting cold is a waste. That is exactly the decision the furnace build was supposed to have, and now the economy states it.

Second: "no downside means no engine" has to be enforced by numbers, not just asserted in a design document. Twin Slug Cannons on Treads had no recoil penalty, stacked damage reduction, and the best sustained damage in the game. Giving the cannon a heat cost that Treads cannot cancel — and capping how far damage reduction can stack — brought it back into the pack.

Current spread, three runs each on Contender:

| build | avg wave |
|---|---|
| furnace + Coolant Cell (the trap) | 1.7 |
| stock / rifle / walker (baseline) | 5.0 |
| governor / intake / drone bays | 6.3 |
| furnace / lance / skates / vent | 7.0 |
| dynamo / mortar / treads | 8.0 |
| capacitor / arc / piston | 8.7 |
| ballast / twin slugs / treads | 9.7 |
| ballast / slug / skates | 10.7 |

Best coherent build to baseline is about 2.1×, best to worst coherent about 1.7×, and the trap sits well under the baseline. A strong build clears all fifteen waves in roughly six minutes of pure combat; a human adds workshop deliberation on top of that.


## Turning toward the survivor-likes

A research pass across Survivor.io, Vampire Survivors, Brotato, Halls of Torment, Deep Rock Galactic: Survivor, Death Must Die and Nova Drift produced a short list of things worth stealing, and one thing worth refusing.

**Refused: full auto-attack.** Survivor.io's entire input surface is a joystick, and that is the correct decision *for Survivor.io*. It is the wrong decision here, because recoil-as-movement is the mechanic this whole game is built on and auto-fire deletes it. The genre has already run this experiment — 20 Minutes Till Dawn, Halls of Torment, Death Must Die and Nova Drift all keep a directed attack — and the resolution they converge on is to **split the arsenal by aim-dependency**. One or two weapons in your hands; everything else fires itself. That way the late-run screen-fill happens in the automatic layer, so the power curve never fights the control scheme.

**Taken: the uniform micro-tree.** Nova Drift and 20 Minutes Till Dawn independently landed on the same shape — Tier I, two Tier II branches, a Tier III capstone. Four nodes. The player learns one structural grammar and then applies it to sixteen subjects, so reading a tree they have never seen costs nothing. Nova Drift's developer wrote about why: at ~150 upgrades the system became unbalanceable, and the fix was mini-trees plus combos as "a middle-ground between deterministic building and rogue-like randomness."

**Taken: combo names printed on the components.** The single best UX device found in the research. Nova Drift's mod cards name the super mod they are half of, so you learn the combinatorial graph by reading the nodes you already own, at the moment you own them. Both 20MTD and Nova Drift shipped with the opposite failure first — synergy lists buried in a menu you cannot open while deciding — and patched toward the card-level hint. We start there.

**Taken: passives that are never a wasted pick.** Vampire Survivors' catalyst items are top-tier stats that happen to also be recipe components, so pursuing an evolution you never complete cannot punish you. Death Must Die guarantees one god who only offers safe passives, specifically so a bad roll degrades a run instead of ending it. Our eight passive trees do that job.

**Taken: a colour reserved for the player.** The most-cited failure of the survivor-like clone wave is losing the player sprite in their own effects. The chassis now carries a light pool and a rim in two colours that nothing else in the game is permitted to use, plus a dark contrast ring underneath. Enemies got a hard black edge and a 25% size bump so that a crowd still reads as individuals.

**Taken: telegraph by clock, silhouette and kinematics, never by per-enemy wind-ups.** Individual telegraphs stop working above roughly fifty enemies on screen. Champions arrive on a schedule you learn across runs; the dangerous things are bigger, differently shaped, or moving anomalously fast.

**Taken: pool-editing verbs.** Banish is on every card. Reroll refills each wave. Vampire Survivors prices Reroll, Skip and Banish as the cheapest things in its entire meta — the player buys consistency before they buy power — and that ordering is deliberate.

### What scrap being the XP gem buys us

The genre's level-up loop is "go get the gems, which are spatially distributed, so levelling speed is a positioning risk." We already had that mechanic: scrap drops where things die, it cools in twenty seconds, and you have to fight forward into your own kills to get it. Making scrap the XP source means the survivor-like hoovering loop and the forward-pressure rule are the same mechanic rather than two systems competing for the floor.

### Open

Convergence is the thing to watch. Survivor.io's most damning review complaint is that fixed recipes plus slot caps solve the optimal build, and the system that makes the first fifty runs great is the same one that kills variety by run two hundred. The defences here are weighted offers rather than a slot cap, sixteen trees against three cards, and banish. Whether that is enough is a playtest question, not a design-document question.
